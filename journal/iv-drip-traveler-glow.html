The Boracay glow routine: how travellers use IV drips to land photo-ready

April 22, 2026 · The Editors · 9 min read

The flight crew on the Manila–Kalibo turn-around have a phrase: landing face. It is not flattering. After a six-hour day of pressure changes, recycled air, and four cups of black coffee, the tissue at the orbital socket loses its quiet, the skin loses two shades of luminosity, and the muscles around the jaw set into the particular stiffness of held tension. Anyone who has ever taken a holiday photograph in the first hour after landing knows what the cabin crew mean.

For travellers headed to Boracay — most of them via the Kalibo Airport gateway — the obvious correction is sleep, water, and a long Hilot. We have nothing against any of these. But we run a clinic, and we'd be remiss not to write about the clinical option, which is the IV drip. This article walks you through what a drip actually does, what we put in the ones we serve, and how to thread the appointment between your boarding gate and the ferry to Caticlan.

What an IV drip is, plainly

An IV drip is a slow, controlled infusion of saline (a salt-water solution at the same concentration as your blood) carrying a measured dose of vitamins, minerals, or in some cases prescription compounds, directly into a vein. Bypassing the digestive system means more of the active compound reaches your tissue — by some measures four to ten times the bioavailability of an oral equivalent. That is the entire pharmacological argument.

The drip itself is forty to sixty minutes in a comfortable chair, with a small needle in the back of the hand or the inside of the elbow. You can read, take a call, sleep, or watch the world go by. There is no recovery time after; you walk out and onto your ferry.

What we put in the drips at Aurum

We offer three core protocols. The variety is intentional; the over-engineering is not.

The Glow Drip — Glutathione + Vitamin C

This is the protocol most travellers ask for and most travellers are right to ask for. Glutathione is the body's master antioxidant — it scavenges the oxidative stress that accumulates from a flight, late nights, alcohol, and the day-to-day inflammation of city living. Vitamin C reactivates oxidised glutathione back into its active form, so the two work together rather than competing.

The cosmetic side-effect of well-supported glutathione is brighter, more even skin. This is not magic, and it is not bleach; what is happening is that melanin production shifts toward the lighter pheomelanin pathway under sustained antioxidant pressure. Over months, the cumulative effect is visible. After a single drip, the effect is largely the absence of dullness, which is its own kind of glow.

Premium Korean glutathione formulations are available on request. The pharmacology is identical; the difference is in carrier purity and price. We will tell you which is suitable for your case.

The Wellness Drip — B-complex + Magnesium + Saline

For the jetlagged. The B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, B12) restore the cofactor pool that keeps mitochondrial energy production running; magnesium relaxes the lining of the vasculature and the bracing of the jaw and shoulders; the saline addresses the dehydration that the cabin pressure has quietly compounded. This is the drip the off-duty pilots ask for, in our experience. It is not glamorous; it is practical.

The Immunity Drip — High-dose Vitamin C + Zinc + Selenium

For travellers who feel the back-of-the-throat tickle as they board, or who are flying out toward a wedding they cannot afford to miss. The dose of Vitamin C in this drip exceeds anything an oral tablet can deliver. Combined with zinc and selenium, it is the protocol the medical literature most consistently associates with shorter, milder upper respiratory illness.

The screening, before any drip

This is the part the wellness Instagram accounts skip. A drip is a prescription medical procedure. Before any line is opened, we run a five-point screen:

  1. Vital signs — blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation. Anomalies pause the drip.
  2. Allergy review, including reactions to prior infusions or contrast media.
  3. Current medication list — particularly anticoagulants and chemotherapy.
  4. Pregnancy or breastfeeding status. Most of our drips are safe during pregnancy; some are not, and we will steer you to the right one.
  5. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency screen for high-dose Vitamin C protocols. (G6PD deficiency is more common in Filipino populations than international averages, and a drip is the wrong way to discover it.)

If any of these screens raises a question, we delay or modify the drip. The answer is never "let's risk it because you have a flight." The clinic is not in the business of triaging your itinerary at the expense of your kidneys.

What a drip will not do

It is not a substitute for sleep, hydration, or eating. It is not an indefinite anti-aging protocol. It is not a treatment for any disease, and any clinic implying otherwise is selling the wrong product. Used episodically, before a wedding or a long flight or a difficult week, it is genuinely useful. Used daily, it is unsupported by evidence and likely a waste of your money.
